# Flow symptom rubric

Five areas. Each carries the symptom statements people actually recognise, and what to
do depending on how bad it is. Use the statements as prompts in conversation — read one
aloud and ask how true it is — rather than as a checklist to work through.

The four-rung response scale for each statement is the same throughout:

- **Not really** — this is fine here.
- **Sometimes** — it happens, but it is not the main pain right now.
- **Often** — this shows up enough that people are frustrated by it.
- **Painfully often** — this is one of our clearest delivery problems.

---

## Flow Time — how long work takes from started to done

Symptoms:

- Work often takes longer than people expected, even when nobody did anything obviously wrong.
- The work is not slow because people are working slowly. It is slow because it waits.

**Red — the place to look first.** Work is taking too long from started to done.
- Look at cycle time for one common type of work.
- Ask where work waits most often.
- Try removing or tightening one review, handoff, approval, or dependency delay.

**Amber — worth watching.** Some work moves well; the slow cases are teaching you something.
- Compare fast and slow items.
- Look for repeated waiting points.
- Pick one delay pattern and run a small experiment.

**Green — a relative strength.** Protect the habits that keep turnaround healthy.
- Keep start and finish boundaries clear.
- Watch for new queues as demand grows.
- Use cycle time only where it improves a real decision.

---

## Flow Load (WIP) — how much work is active at once

Symptoms:

- We have too much started and not enough finished.
- People are pulled across too many priorities, meetings, support requests, or side quests.

**Red.** Too much work is active at once.
- Count active work before starting more.
- Review work right to left: finish, unblock, then start.
- Try a temporary WIP limit or an explicit pause/stop rule.

**Amber.** Not overloaded everywhere, but focus is leaking.
- Look for work that keeps carrying over.
- Name what will slow down before accepting new work.
- Protect focus for the work closest to done.

**Green.** Keep it that way by resisting the temptation to fill every gap.
- Keep active work visible.
- Watch for creeping WIP.
- Use WIP as an early warning signal, not a utilisation target.

---

## Throughput — how much the system usually finishes

Symptoms:

- When we plan, we are not sure how much we can realistically finish.
- What we finish swings a lot from week to week or sprint to sprint.

**Red.** No reliable feel for how much the system can finish.
- Look at how many items actually finished in recent weeks or sprints.
- Compare plans to completed work without blame.
- Use recent throughput to make the next plan less wishful.

**Amber.** Usable but uneven. Forecasting improves if you understand the swings.
- Separate normal variation from special causes.
- Look at interruptions, oversized work, and dependency-heavy items.
- Use completion history as a conversation starter, not a target.

**Green.** A decent baseline for near-term planning.
- Keep forecasts grounded in recent completion patterns.
- Make uncertainty visible.
- Avoid turning throughput into a pressure metric.

## Flow Distribution — whether the right mix of work is getting attention

This is the area most often missing from a flow-metrics conversation, and often the one
that explains why the other four look fine and the business still is not moving.

Symptoms:

- The work that gets done is not always the work we most need done.
- Unplanned work, support work, defects, or escalations quietly eat more capacity than people realise.

**Red.** The system is busy, but not enough of the right work is getting through.
- Look at the mix of finished work: planned, unplanned, defects, support, discovery, delivery, risk, improvement.
- Ask what important work is being crowded out.
- Make one explicit capacity tradeoff for the next two weeks.

**Amber.** The mix may be drifting away from what matters most.
- Review the mix of completed work, not just the volume.
- Separate urgent work from important work.
- Name the work type you want to protect.

**Green.** You have some control over the mix.
- Keep hidden demand visible.
- Watch for unplanned work creeping up.
- Use distribution to protect strategic work, not to micromanage teams.

---

## Work Item Aging — whether active work gets old before anyone notices

Symptoms:

- Work looks fine in status updates, then suddenly it is late, blocked, or in trouble.
- We do not have a simple trigger for asking, "why is this still open?"

**Red.** Work is getting old before anyone acts.
- Review the oldest active items first.
- Ask why this is still open, before it becomes a crisis.
- Create a simple trigger for swarming, splitting, unblocking, or escalating stale work.

**Amber.** Some stale work is visible, but probably too late.
- Add an aging check to an existing daily or weekly review.
- Look for items that are old but still reported as green.
- Agree what action to take when work crosses the age threshold.

**Green.** Stale work is caught early enough.
- Keep the age review lightweight.
- Use age as a trigger for action, not a reporting burden.
- Watch for aging patterns when WIP grows.

## Interpreting the overall picture

**Several areas red.** This is not a sign that people need to work harder. It is a sign
that the system needs to be easier to see and manage. Do not launch a metrics programme.
Pick the lowest area, one real workflow, one simple signal, two weeks, and use it to make
a better decision about what to finish, start, stop, split, unblock, or protect.

**A few areas amber.** This is where flow metrics are most useful — not as a dashboard,
but as a way to see one recurring friction point more clearly. Pick the area with the
biggest pain, inspect it for two weeks, change one policy or review habit.

**Mostly green.** A healthy picture does not mean ignore flow; it means stay lightweight.
Use the lowest-scoring area as an early warning signal. Add a metric only where it helps
a decision that already needs making, and avoid turning flow into another reporting
ritual.
